In one paragraph

We are moving our contact database and all marketing email from Mailchimp into HubSpot. The migration is 73% complete and finishes in the next two weeks. Mailchimp stays switched on for one more month after that as a safety net, so if anything did not come across we find it while we still have the original. In October we add HeyReach to run LinkedIn outreach on the same contact database, alongside the email work. The end state is one system holding our contacts, our email, and our pipeline, instead of three systems that do not talk to each other.

Why we are doing this

Three problems, all of which cost us revenue today.

1. Our email was not reaching inboxes

Plain English: sender reputation

Every company that sends email has a reputation score held by Gmail, Outlook and the other inbox providers. It is based mostly on how often recipients mark your mail as spam. A good reputation means your email lands in the inbox. A bad one means it goes to spam or is refused at the door, and that applies to every email from your domain, including invoices and renewal notices.

27-30%Our Mailchimp spam complaint rate
Under 0.1%What inbox providers expect
213,000Dead contacts on the list

We were running a spam complaint rate roughly 300 times higher than the accepted threshold. That is not a marketing inconvenience, it is the reason campaigns underperformed regardless of what the message said. We were sending to a very large list where most people had stopped engaging years ago, and every send made the reputation worse.

What this means in practice

Fixing the list is not optional and it cannot be fixed inside Mailchimp, because the damage attaches to the sending history. Moving to HubSpot with a cleaned list and a disciplined warm-up is how we rebuild deliverability. It is the single highest-value thing in this project.

2. Our data lives in three places that do not talk

Contacts sit in ORO. Email sits in Mailchimp. Neither connects to the other, so when a customer signs up we cannot see which marketing touch brought them in. We have been unable to answer the most basic commercial question: which of our marketing spend produces customers.

After the move, HubSpot holds contacts, email and pipeline in one place. ORO continues as our leads tracker feeding downstream systems, so nothing in the existing operational flow breaks.

3. Email was one person doing one-off sends

Today every campaign is built by hand, one at a time, with no repeatable segments and no automation. There is no way to send the right message to the right kind of customer without rebuilding the audience from scratch each time. HubSpot gives us a fixed library of audience segments that gets reused, which makes sends faster, more targeted, and far harder to get wrong.

What a "marketing contact" is, and why the number matters

Plain English: marketing contacts

HubSpot charges based on how many contacts you are allowed to email, not how many records you store. A record you keep for reference is free. A record you can send marketing email to is billable. So the discipline is to be deliberate about who actually belongs on the mailable list.

We started with 231,889 raw records out of Mailchimp. Rather than move all of them and pay for the privilege, every record was classified first.

Why this matters commercially

Sending less, to better-chosen people, is what rebuilds deliverability. A smaller engaged list outperforms a large dead one, and it costs less to hold.

The safety net: one month running both systems

The main risk in any migration is discovering three weeks later that something did not come across.

So we are not switching Mailchimp off when HubSpot goes live. Mailchimp stays active through late October, roughly a month of the two systems running side by side, while HubSpot takes over sending. That gives us a working original to check against for as long as it takes to be confident.

What we are checking during the parallel month

  • Every contact that existed in Mailchimp is accounted for in HubSpot, or was deliberately excluded with a reason.
  • Unsubscribes and spam complaints are being honoured. Nobody who opted out gets contacted.
  • Deliverability on the new platform is improving, measured on complaint rate and bounce rate, not on opens.
  • Historical engagement carried across, so we can still tell an engaged customer from a dormant one.
  • Reporting reconciles. The numbers HubSpot shows match what actually went out.
The decommission test

Mailchimp is switched off when all five of those check out and HubSpot has sent successfully for four consecutive weeks inside safe complaint thresholds. If any one of them fails, we extend rather than push ahead. The cost of another month of Mailchimp is trivial next to the cost of damaging the sending domain again.

Phase 2: LinkedIn outreach, from October

We add LinkedIn as a second outbound channel, run centrally, in parallel with the email validation work.

Why this can run alongside the Mailchimp parallel month

LinkedIn is a separate channel with separate risk. It does not touch our email sending domain, so nothing about it can affect the deliverability rebuild happening at the same time. The two workstreams share a contact database and nothing else.

We are bringing in a tool called HeyReach. The reason we chose it over the alternatives is structural rather than a feature comparison.

Plain English: why this is run like an agency

Most LinkedIn outreach tools assume each sales rep logs in and runs their own campaigns. That fails in practice, because reps are measured on closing, not on campaign admin, so the campaigns quietly stop. HeyReach lets one person operate campaigns centrally across several LinkedIn accounts at once. Reps lend their professional identity, which is what makes the outreach credible, and receive qualified replies as handoffs. They run nothing themselves.

What we get out of it

  • Email that arrives. A clean list and a rebuilt sender reputation, so campaigns are judged on the message rather than on whether they were delivered.
  • An answer to "what is marketing producing." One system connecting the first touch to the closed customer, which we cannot do today at all.
  • Targeting by who the customer actually is. Distributors, MROs, airlines and defense contractors receive messages relevant to their business instead of one generic send.
  • A second outbound channel that runs without adding headcount and without adding work to the sales team.
  • Three vendors becoming one platform, with a clear operational owner.
